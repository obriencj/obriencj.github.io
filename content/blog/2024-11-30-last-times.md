"All things must end." Observable, sensible. For every element of your
life, for each repeated series of actions, there will be a final
instance. 2024 brought a series of these in close proximity, and I feel
compelled now to spend some time focusing on the impermanence.

<!-- more -->

I am not adverse to novelty, or new experiences. I've been accused of
this more than a few times in my life, but it's an incorrect
interpretation of my habits. If I find something in my life that I
enjoy, I will return to it. If something goes well, why not do it
again? Why not return to that moment of happiness? So I have a bit of
a name for being a creature of repetition. It's simply that I want
more of a good thing whenever I can get it.

This year, a number of good things came to an end. Repeated series
terminated. I was not ready for the last times, for the final
instances.

My grandmother passed away this year. One week after finishing my
week-long bike ride. I visited her for mothers day. Then the next
weekend I spent the night at my partner's house, and woke early to a
text message. Nan has left us before the sun rose. I found myself
sobbing, and she held me as I wept. My nan had raised me before my
parents could wed, she was my delightful visit, my caretaker, my
rolemodel for what the best a person could be looked like. Forgiving,
sweet, loving, caring, perfect.

Nan was a great cook, but one dinner stood above all others. Nan's
pot-pie was an event. It was a family favorite, to the point where it
was almost a religious event. "Nan is making pot-pie," would get
people moving, involved. Now, to be clear, this was Pennsylvania dutch
pot-pie, which is more of a beef and dumplings than an actual "pie."
Thick soft dough, boiled into noodles in beef broth. Potatos cut into
eighths, parsley. And of course the beef itself, which fell apart on
the fork. Salted, peppered, it was filling and warm. The happiness of
my childhood is wrapped up in the flavor of that dinner, the smell of
it on the stove. The specific plates it was served in, more like a
shallow bowl almost. There were always second helpings, sometimes
thirds. Nan gave the recipe to my wife when I got married, but I don't
believe she ever made it at home.

At some point, not too long before Nan left us, there was a last time
for that pot-pie. I didn't know it then, how could I? Or maybe I
suspected, but refused to acknowledge how frail she'd become, how
tired she seemed.

I won't ever, in this life, have that meal again. The most special,
and important dinner, remembered from when I was in elementary school
up through my mid 40s. My own daughter is 20 years old now, and it was
even a part of her life.

Pot pie was one just one of the many ways that Nan told us she loved
us. I'll miss hugging her, hearing her voice, the way she joked, her
stories of us in our infancies. All of those are gone, disappeared and
converted into nothing more than memories.

But this is not just about my Nana.

The day prior, in a bizarre coincidence, Capital Club 16 shut its
doors for the last time. I had started eating there after my
divorce. I needed to get out of the house, so I began experimenting
with coming downtown. Coffee shops, bars, and restaurants. Their food
was german, so of course I was happy to be there.

I used to go there twice a week. I knew the staff, knew the owner. He
hosted the Distinguished Gentleman's Ride a few times.

My partner and I would go there for a drink, and for dinner. We'd sit
at the bar together and joke with the people who worked there. Friends
through exposure. I'd meet my other friends there for outings and
lunches. It was central to the experience of getting out of the house,
of going downtown. Of escaping the loneliness of adult life, and
replacing that sadness with sounds and smiles and good food.



I mourn this finality. I am hurt by the termination of this series.
